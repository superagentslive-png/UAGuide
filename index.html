Telephone Consumer Protection Act
47 U.S.C. §227 · The TCPA is different from the FDCPA. The FDCPA is about who is collecting the debt. The TCPA is about how they are contacting the consumer. This distinction changes everything about who we can go after.
$500Per call / text violation
$1,500Per willful violation
No capDamages stack per call
The single most important thing to understand
TCPA is not just for third-party debt collectors.
The FDCPA only reaches third-party collectors. The TCPA can apply to original creditors, third-party collectors, debt buyers, collection law firms, vendors, call centers, servicers, and anyone calling or texting on their behalf — if they use covered technology without valid consent or after consent has been revoked. The statute restricts calls and texts using an automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS), artificial voice, or prerecorded voice. TCPA applies based on technology + consent. Not collector status.

What triggers TCPA coverage

The TCPA does not regulate all calls. It regulates the technology used to place them. Two independent triggers exist — either is sufficient:

Trigger 1
Automated Telephone Dialing System (ATDS)

Equipment with the capacity to store or produce telephone numbers using a random or sequential number generator and to dial those numbers automatically. In practice: robodialers, predictive dialers, and automated calling platforms.

Signs your client is being called by an ATDS: dead air or delay before anyone speaks, identical robotic opener on every call, multiple calls per day from the same number, calls resume immediately after hanging up.
Trigger 2
Prerecorded or Artificial Voice

Any call or voicemail using a prerecorded message or AI-generated voice. The FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices on robocalls qualify as "artificial or prerecorded voice" under the TCPA — closing a potential loophole.

This trigger does not require an ATDS. A manually dialed call with a prerecorded message or AI voice still violates the TCPA. The technology in the message matters, not just the dialing method.
⚠

The Facebook v. Duguid limitation (2021): The Supreme Court narrowed the ATDS definition — equipment must use a random or sequential number generator to qualify. This means "they called me" is no longer enough. We need evidence of the specific technology used. Manual human calls are a much weaker TCPA claim. The prerecorded/artificial voice trigger is separate and unaffected by Duguid.

Vicarious liability — going after the creditor for their vendor's calls

This is one of the most powerful TCPA theories and one that teams often miss. A creditor or collector can be held liable for TCPA violations committed by their third-party call centers and dialer vendors if they directed, controlled, or ratified the calling conduct.

ℹ

What this means in practice: Even if a third-party offshore call center physically dialed your client, the original creditor who hired and directed that call center can be the named defendant. Don't dismiss a case just because the caller ID shows an unfamiliar vendor name — follow it up the chain.

The consent framework — how violations are created

How consent is established

When a consumer provides their cell number on the original credit application or account agreement, courts treat that as prior express consent for autodialed calls related to that account. This is the creditor's primary defense for early collection calls — and why the revocation step is so critical.

Why consent doesn't transfer on debt sale

When a creditor sells or assigns debt to a third-party collector, the consumer's original consent does not automatically follow. This is why third-party and debt buyer cases are often the cleanest TCPA violations — the consent chain is broken at the point of assignment.

The key TCPA trigger: consent revocation

⚠

TCPA vs. FDCPA revocation — a critical operational difference: Under the FDCPA, a cease-communication request must be in writing. Under the TCPA, an oral "stop calling me" on a recorded line is sufficient to revoke consent. If your client told a collector on a call to stop calling — and they kept calling with an autodialer — TCPA violations are already accruing. Document the date of that conversation.

Model revocation language — include in attorney representation letter
"I revoke any and all consent — express or implied — for you, your agents, vendors, affiliates, servicers, or anyone calling or texting on your behalf to call or text my cell phone using an autodialer, automated system, prerecorded voice, artificial voice, AI voice, predictive dialer, or any automated texting system. Do not call or text this number except as legally permitted."

Evidence we need — not just "they called"

Was it a robocall? Dead air or pause before anyone spoke?
Prerecorded voicemail? Same exact message left every time?
Automated or AI voice? Did it sound robotic, unnaturally smooth, or scripted?
Automated texts? Short-code texts, instant responses, identical message strings?
Did client revoke consent? When, how, and to whom? Verbal or written?
Did calls continue after revocation? Count every post-revocation contact.
Who was the caller? Creditor, collector, or vendor calling on their behalf?
How many total violations? Pull full call log from the phone carrier.

The California layer: Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act

The federal FDCPA leaves a gap: original creditors collecting their own debts aren't covered. California closed that gap with the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (Cal. Civil Code §1788 et seq.), which applies FDCPA-equivalent conduct rules to all debt collectors in California — including original creditors.

FDCPA — Introduction
Fair Debt Collection Practices Act
15 U.S.C. §§1692–1692p · The federal law governing how third-party debt collectors may contact, communicate with, and pursue consumers.
$1,000Statutory damages per case
1 yearStatute of limitations
+feesAttorney fees if you win

Why this law exists

1
Eliminate abusive collection practices
▾

Congress found that abusive practices contribute to bankruptcies, marital instability, and job loss. The statute is designed to stop these practices at scale.

2
Give consumers tools to dispute and verify debt
▾

The law creates a structured validation system — consumers have a right to demand proof of a debt's validity before a collector can continue pursuing it.

3
Protect reputable collectors from unfair competition
▾

The FDCPA levels the playing field by ensuring bad-actor collectors can't undercut ethical ones by relying on harassment tactics.

ℹ

FDCPA is a strict liability statute. Intent is not required for most violations. The conduct itself is what matters. The bona fide error defense exists but is narrow — repeated identical "errors" are not mistakes, they are policy.

§1692a — Definitions
Who Is Covered
The FDCPA only applies to "debt collectors" pursuing "debts" as specifically defined. Getting this right at intake is step one — if FDCPA doesn't apply, pivot to TCPA and Rosenthal analysis immediately.

Who qualifies as a "debt collector"

✓
Third-party collection agencies
▾

Any person or entity whose principal business is collecting debts owed to another, or who regularly collects debts owed to another.

✓
Debt buyers — courts treat them as debt collectors
▾

Courts have consistently held that debt buyers still qualify as "debt collectors" under the FDCPA because the debt was in default when purchased.

✓
Collection law firms
▾

Attorneys and law firms whose principal business is debt collection are expressly covered. All collection law firm letters must include the mini-Miranda disclosure.

?
Original creditor using another name — possible FDCPA
▾

If an original creditor uses a subsidiary name or trade name that implies a third party is collecting, courts may apply the FDCPA.

Who is excluded from FDCPA

✗
Original creditors collecting their own debt under their own name
▾

Chase calling about a Chase account. However, TCPA still applies if they're using an autodialer or prerecorded voice. And Rosenthal applies for California clients.

✗
Creditors collecting before default
▾

Auto finance companies and mortgage servicers collecting payments on active, non-defaulted accounts are excluded. The FDCPA is triggered after default.

§1692b–c
Communication Rules
The FDCPA strictly governs when, where, and how a collector may contact a consumer. Many of the most provable violations are communication violations — and they overlap with TCPA claims.

Time and place restrictions — §1692c

✗
No calls before 8:00 AM or after 9:00 PM — consumer's local time
▾

Collectors must account for time zones. An early-morning or late-evening call is a per se violation. Have clients log all call times with dates.

✗
No contact at the workplace if employer prohibits it
▾

The client telling the collector "don't call me at work" is sufficient notice — no formal employer policy required.

✗
No direct contact once consumer is represented by an attorney — §1692c(a)(2)
▾

The moment a client enrolls and we notify the collector of legal representation, all direct consumer contact must stop. Any direct call to the client after that date is a violation.

§1692c(a)(2): A debt collector may not communicate with a consumer "if the debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with respect to such debt and has knowledge of, or can readily ascertain, such attorney's name and address."
⚠

UA Law action item: Send attorney representation notice on Day 1. Date-stamp delivery. Any calls to the client after that date are clean FDCPA violations — and if the calls are autodialed, TCPA violations too.

✗
Cease communication obligation after written request
▾

A written cease-and-desist stops all collection communications. The only permitted contacts after: (1) confirming that collection is ceasing, or (2) notifying of specific intended legal action.

!

Written vs. oral: Under FDCPA, the cease-communication request must be in writing. Under TCPA, an oral "stop calling me" on a recorded call is sufficient. File both claims and let each statute's standard apply independently.

Contacting third parties — §1692b

  • May contact third parties only to locate the consumer
  • Must not reveal the existence of the debt to any third party
  • Cannot contact the same third party more than once unless that person requests it
  • Cannot identify themselves as a debt collector unless specifically asked
§1692d, §1692e, §1692f
Prohibited Practices
Three separate sections — harassment/abuse (§d), false representations (§e), and unfair practices (§f). Each is an independent cause of action. Stack them where the facts support it.

§1692d — Harassment and abuse

✗
Repeated or continuous calls to annoy — §1692d(5)
▾

Regulation F (CFPB, 2021) created a rebuttable presumption: more than 7 calls within 7 days, or calling within 7 days of a conversation, is presumptively a violation.

✗
Threats of violence or harm
▾

Any threat of violence or harm to the consumer, their reputation, or their property. Clients should document exact language used.

✗
Obscene or profane language
▾

Clients should record calls where legally permitted, or document exact words immediately after the call.

§1692e — False or misleading representations

✗
Misrepresenting the amount or character of the debt — §1692e(2)
▾

Claiming a debt is larger than it actually is, including unauthorized fees or interest. Also applies to misrepresenting a time-barred debt as legally enforceable.

✗
Falsely implying they are an attorney — §1692e(3)
▾

Representing or implying a communication is from an attorney when it is not. Law firm letterhead used by non-attorney collectors is the classic form of this violation.

✗
Threatening legal action not intended — §1692e(5)
▾

Threatening to sue, garnish wages, or seize property when the collector has no legal basis or actual intent. One of the most common violations.

✗
Threat of arrest or criminal prosecution — §1692e(4)
▾

Consumer debts are civil matters. No criminal prosecution is possible for nonpayment. Any suggestion of arrest is per se false.

✗
Failure to identify as a debt collector — §1692e(11)
▾

The mini-Miranda: every initial written and oral communication must disclose that the caller is a debt collector attempting to collect a debt. Absence is a per se violation.

§1692f — Unfair practices

✗
Collecting unauthorized amounts — §1692f(1)
▾

Collecting any amount — interest, fees, charges, expenses — not expressly authorized by the original agreement or permitted by law.

✗
Filing suit in an improper venue — §1692i
▾

A collector may only sue in the judicial district where the consumer resides or where the contract was signed. Suing in a distant court is an independent FDCPA violation.

§1692g — Full Statutory Analysis
Validation of Debts
The cornerstone of the debt validation program. §1692g creates a mandatory disclosure and dispute framework. Failure to comply is a per se FDCPA violation. Know this section cold.
★

Why this matters to us: The §1692g notice is the statutory foundation for the debt validation letters your clients send. It also creates independent violations when collectors fail to send the notice, ignore a written dispute, or continue collecting during the dispute window.

The 30-day validation timeline

D0
Day 0 — First contact
The collector makes initial communication. The 5-day clock starts.
D5
Within 5 days — Validation notice required
Must send a written notice with all five required disclosures. Missing any single element is a per se violation.
D30
Within 30 days — Consumer's dispute window
Consumer has 30 days to send a written dispute. Collection may continue but cannot "overshadow" the consumer's dispute rights.
→
After written dispute — Collection suspended
All collection activity must stop until the collector obtains and mails verification of the debt.
§1692g(a)Notice of debt — required contents
  1. The amount of the debt
  2. The name of the creditor to whom the debt is owed
  3. A statement that unless the consumer disputes within thirty days, the debt will be assumed to be valid
  4. A statement that if disputed in writing within thirty days, the collector will obtain verification and mail it
  5. A statement that upon written request within thirty days, the collector will provide the name and address of the original creditor

§1692k — Civil liability
Remedies & Damages
Strict liability statute — intent is not required for most violations. What matters is the conduct itself.
$1,000Max statutory per lawsuit
No capActual damages
MandatoryAttorney fees if you win
$
Actual damages — no ceiling
▾

Includes emotional distress, lost wages, out-of-pocket costs, and any other actual harm.

$
Statutory damages — up to $1,000 per lawsuit
▾

No actual harm required. The $1,000 is per lawsuit, not per violation — which is why TCPA is filed alongside FDCPA.

$
Attorney fees and costs — mandatory if plaintiff wins
▾

The FDCPA mandates an award of reasonable attorney fees and costs to a successful plaintiff. This fee-shifting is what makes FDCPA cases economically viable.

15 U.S.C. §1692k(c) — Bona fide error defense
A debt collector may not be held liable if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error.
⚠

Counter-strategy: Establish a pattern. If the same collector has repeated the same conduct across multiple accounts, the bona fide error defense collapses — repeated identical "errors" are policy, not mistakes.

State-Level Overlays
Mirroring States
Federal FDCPA is the floor. Mirroring-state laws are the state-level overlays that may apply more broadly — sometimes against original creditors the federal statute cannot touch.
The clean explanation
Mirroring states are where state law gives consumers FDCPA-like protections — even when the federal FDCPA may not apply.
These laws often mirror FDCPA rules but some states go further by applying those rules to original creditors. You need to verify each state separately before filing. The state of client residence at the time of the conduct is the controlling factor.

  • If yes — did the notice contain all 5 required elements?
  • Was the dispute right "overshadowed" by urgent short-deadline payment demands?
  • Has client sent a written dispute? If yes, did collection stop immediately?
  • Did the collector resume collection before mailing verification?

Step 3 — Communication violations §1692c

  • Send attorney representation notice Day 1 — date-stamp delivery. All direct consumer contact after that is a violation.
  • Any calls before 8:00 AM or after 9:00 PM in the client's time zone?
  • Any calls to the workplace after client requested otherwise?
  • Any contact with third parties that revealed the existence of the debt?

Step 4 — TCPA analysis

  • Signs of autodialer: dead air before agent speaks, identical robotic opener, rapid-fire call cadence?
  • Any prerecorded or AI-voice messages or voicemails?
  • Any automated texts (short code, instant response, identical message strings)?
  • Did client ever verbally say "stop calling"? On a recorded line? Document date.
  • Has attorney representation notice been sent? Count every call after that date.
  • Count total calls after first revocation — each post-revocation autodialed call = $500–$1,500
  • If debt was sold/assigned: consent did not transfer — collector may be calling without any valid consent.

Step 5 — Harassment and false statements §1692d/e

  • More than 7 calls in any 7-day period? (Regulation F presumption of harassment)
  • Any calls within 7 days of an actual conversation?
  • Any threats of arrest, criminal prosecution, or wage garnishment before a judgment?
  • Any claim that the caller is an attorney when they are not?
  • Any fees or interest added beyond what the original agreement permits?
  • Any failure to identify as a debt collector in initial communication?

Step 6 — Documentation to collect

  • Full call log from client's phone carrier (incoming calls with exact dates and times)
  • All written letters, emails, or notices received from the collector
  • All voicemails saved — do not delete, note whether prerecorded or live
  • Screenshots of any texts including short-code messages
  • Copy of original credit agreement if available
  • Account statements showing balance vs. what the collector is demanding
  • Date of attorney representation notice and confirmed delivery method
  • Any prior verbal revocations the client made — dates, phone numbers called, what they said

Fair Credit Reporting Act
The FCRA governs who can access consumer credit reports, what information can be reported, and what happens when that information is wrong. Violations can come from three different actors — the statute, duties, and damages path depend on which actor failed.

Why FCRA matters for debt relief clients
The debt they enrolled is almost certainly being reported. How it's being reported — and whether that reporting is accurate — is a separate set of claims.
Creditors and collectors who report inaccurate information — or fail to investigate and correct it after a dispute — create furnisher violations under §1681s-2(b). These are independent of FDCPA claims. Every debt relief client is a potential FCRA client.

The two subsections — why only one matters for private suits

§1681s-2(a) — Duty to Report Accurately
  • Prohibits furnishing info known to be inaccurate
  • Requires correction after furnisher learns of an error
  • No private right of action — enforced only by FTC/CFPB
§1681s-2(b) — Post-Dispute Investigation Duties ★
  • Triggered when CRA notifies furnisher of a consumer dispute
  • Must conduct reasonable investigation of the disputed info
  • Must report results back to the CRA
  • Private right of action exists — this is the lawsuit hook

Q4 — What amount or frequency of calls becomes a violation?

📞
The CFPB benchmark — Regulation F

For third-party debt collectors, the CFPB debt collection rule creates a key benchmark: a collector is presumed to violate the rule if it calls about a particular debt more than 7 times within 7 consecutive days, or calls again within 7 days after having a phone conversation with the consumer about that debt.

!

Frequency is only one lane. A single call can still matter if it happens after revocation, at work after the collector knows work calls aren't allowed, to a third party, before 8 a.m., after 9 p.m., or includes threats or deception. Flag the conduct — don't try to classify it.

Q5 — What types of violations exist across calls, texts, credit, and letters?

📞
Calls — what is prohibited
▾
  • Excessive calls (more than 7 in 7 days presumed violation)
  • Calls before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m.
  • Calls after cease-and-desist
  • Calls after revocation of consent
  • Calls to work after being told not to call there
  • Robocalls or prerecorded calls without proper consent
  • Threatening, abusive, or deceptive calls
💬
Texts — what is prohibited
▾
  • Texts after STOP / revocation
  • Automated texts without proper consent
  • Texts that disclose debt information
  • Repeated texts that become harassment
FCC confirmed: consumers can revoke consent to robocalls and robotexts through reasonable methods, and revocation can apply across call and text channels.
📊
Credit reporting — what is prohibited
▾
  • Incorrect balance or wrong status
  • Duplicate account reporting
  • Re-aged debt (restarting the 7-year clock)
  • Reporting after settlement incorrectly
  • Failure to investigate after dispute
  • Failure to mark account as disputed
📄
Letters / emails — what is prohibited
▾
  • False threats or misleading settlement language
  • Wrong amount or unauthorized fees
  • Missing or defective validation information
  • Confusing or deceptive deadlines
👥
Third-party contacts — a major red flag
▾

Debt collectors generally cannot contact family, friends, employers, or references to discuss the debt. They may have limited ability to contact third parties to locate the consumer — but they generally cannot disclose the debt or use third-party contact as pressure.

AM script — third-party contact
"If anyone contacted your family, employer, references, or friends — please tell us who they contacted, when, what number they called from, what they said, and whether they mentioned the debt."

Q6 — What is the average value or cost per violation?

FDCPA
Usually applies to third-party debt collectors. Statutory damages up to $1,000 per lawsuit, plus actual damages and attorney's fees. Note: the $1,000 is per case, not per violation — it doesn't stack.
TCPA
Call and text focused. Statutory damages of $500 per violation, and up to $1,500 for willful or knowing violations. Critically — TCPA damages stack per call. 30 calls = up to $45,000.
FCRA
Credit reporting cases are fact-heavy. Negligent violations allow actual damages and attorney's fees. Willful violations may add statutory damages ($100–$1,000 per violation) and punitive damages.
Manager script — when clients ask about value
"The value depends on the law, the evidence, the company involved, the number of events, and the harm. Our job is to preserve the evidence and get it reviewed."

Q8 — Do violations vary by state?

⚖
Yes — state matters. Always capture the client's state.
▾

Federal laws (FDCPA, TCPA, FCRA) create the baseline. State laws may add additional protection. Some states have debt collection laws that cover more conduct than federal law — including coverage of original creditors that FDCPA misses.

California's Rosenthal Act is the most important example for our portfolio: it extends FDCPA-equivalent rules to original creditors. Florida, Texas, North Carolina, and others also have state-level overlays. See the Mirroring States page for a full breakdown.

Q9 — Can they call my family members, employer, or references?

🚩
Usually a major red flag — escalate immediately
▾

A debt collector generally cannot contact family, friends, employers, or references to discuss the debt. They may have limited ability to contact third parties to locate the consumer — but they generally cannot:

  • Disclose the existence of the debt to anyone other than the consumer
  • Use third-party contact as a pressure tactic
  • Contact the consumer at work if they know or have reason to know the employer prohibits those calls
  • Contact the same third party more than once (unless that person asks)

Q11 — What dates and times can creditors or collectors call?

🕐
The federal time-of-day rule for debt collectors
▾
  • Not before 8:00 a.m. in the consumer's local time
  • Not after 9:00 p.m. in the consumer's local time
  • Not at a time or place they know is inconvenient
  • Not at work if they know the employer prohibits those calls
  • Must honor instructions about when and where not to contact the consumer
!

Don't let clients — or yourself — think only time-of-day matters. A 2 p.m. call can still be a problem if it is after revocation, after a cease-and-desist, to an employer, to a family member, harassing, deceptive, automated without consent, or connected to a disputed or misreported debt.
