Telephone Consumer Protection Act
47 U.S.C. §227 · The TCPA is different from the FDCPA. The FDCPA is about who is collecting the debt. The TCPA is about how they are contacting the consumer. This distinction changes everything about who we can go after.
$500Per call / text violation
$1,500Per willful violation
No capDamages stack per call
The single most important thing to understand
TCPA is not just for third-party debt collectors.
The FDCPA only reaches third-party collectors. The TCPA can apply to original creditors, third-party collectors, debt buyers, collection law firms, vendors, call centers, servicers, and anyone calling or texting on their behalf — if they use covered technology without valid consent or after consent has been revoked. The statute restricts calls and texts using an automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS), artificial voice, or prerecorded voice. TCPA applies based on technology + consent. Not collector status.

What triggers TCPA coverage

The TCPA does not regulate all calls. It regulates the technology used to place them. Two independent triggers exist — either is sufficient:

Trigger 1
Automated Telephone Dialing System (ATDS)

Equipment with the capacity to store or produce telephone numbers using a random or sequential number generator and to dial those numbers automatically. In practice: robodialers, predictive dialers, and automated calling platforms.

Signs your client is being called by an ATDS: dead air or delay before anyone speaks, identical robotic opener on every call, multiple calls per day from the same number, calls resume immediately after hanging up.
Trigger 2
Prerecorded or Artificial Voice

Any call or voicemail using a prerecorded message or AI-generated voice. The FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices on robocalls qualify as "artificial or prerecorded voice" under the TCPA — closing a potential loophole.

This trigger does not require an ATDS. A manually dialed call with a prerecorded message or AI voice still violates the TCPA. The technology in the message matters, not just the dialing method.
⚠

The Facebook v. Duguid limitation (2021): The Supreme Court narrowed the ATDS definition — equipment must use a random or sequential number generator to qualify. This means "they called me" is no longer enough. We need evidence of the specific technology used. Manual human calls are a much weaker TCPA claim. The prerecorded/artificial voice trigger is separate and unaffected by Duguid.

Vicarious liability — going after the creditor for their vendor's calls

This is one of the most powerful TCPA theories and one that teams often miss. A creditor or collector can be held liable for TCPA violations committed by their third-party call centers and dialer vendors if they directed, controlled, or ratified the calling conduct. The FCC has confirmed this framework.

ℹ

What this means in practice: Even if a third-party offshore call center physically dialed your client, the original creditor who hired and directed that call center can be the named defendant. The creditor's contract with the vendor, call scripts provided, and supervision of the program all go to the control question. Don't dismiss a case just because the caller ID shows an unfamiliar vendor name — follow it up the chain.

The consent framework — how violations are created

TCPA liability almost always comes down to two questions: did the caller have valid consent, and was it properly revoked? Your team needs to understand both sides.

How consent is established

When a consumer provides their cell number on the original credit application or account agreement, courts treat that as prior express consent for autodialed calls related to that account. This is the creditor's primary defense for early collection calls — and why the revocation step is so critical to create violations.

Why consent doesn't transfer on debt sale

When a creditor sells or assigns debt to a third-party collector, the consumer's original consent does not automatically follow. The collector is calling without clean consent. This is why third-party and debt buyer cases are often the cleanest TCPA violations in our portfolio — the consent chain is broken at the point of assignment.

The key TCPA trigger: consent revocation

A consumer may revoke consent through any reasonable method that clearly expresses they do not want further calls or texts. Current FCC rules require revocation requests to be honored within a reasonable time — not to exceed 10 business days. Every autodialed or prerecorded call after valid revocation is a standalone violation.

⚠

TCPA vs. FDCPA revocation — a critical operational difference: Under the FDCPA, a cease-communication request must be in writing to trigger §1692c obligations. Under the TCPA, an oral "stop calling me" on a recorded line is sufficient to revoke consent. If your client told a collector on a call to stop calling — and they kept calling with an autodialer — TCPA violations are already accruing. Document the date of that conversation.

The goal is not just to stop the calls. The goal is to create a clear, documented record of revocation so that every subsequent autodialed contact becomes a provable violation. Use this language in your revocation notice:

Model revocation language — include in attorney representation letter
"I revoke any and all consent — express or implied — for you, your agents, vendors, affiliates, servicers, or anyone calling or texting on your behalf to call or text my cell phone using an autodialer, automated system, prerecorded voice, artificial voice, AI voice, predictive dialer, or any automated texting system. Do not call or text this number except as legally permitted."

Evidence we need — not just "they called"

After Facebook v. Duguid, we cannot rely on call volume alone to establish ATDS use. We need to identify the specific technology. Ask clients about every one of these:

Was it a robocall? Dead air or pause before anyone spoke?
Prerecorded voicemail? Same exact message left every time?
Automated or AI voice? Did it sound robotic, unnaturally smooth, or scripted?
Automated texts? Short-code texts, instant responses, identical message strings?
Did client revoke consent? When, how, and to whom? Verbal or written?
Did calls continue after revocation? Count every post-revocation contact.
Who was the caller? Creditor, collector, or vendor calling on their behalf?
How many total violations? Pull full call log from the phone carrier.

Do not ask only "Did they call after revocation?" Ask "How did they call?" The answer determines which statute fires and how strong the claim is.

TCPA damages stack per violation. Every post-revocation robocall or robotext is a separate item in the damages calculation. This is the fundamental difference from the FDCPA's $1,000 per-case cap.

The California layer: Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act

The federal FDCPA leaves a gap: original creditors collecting their own debts aren't covered. California closed that gap with the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (Cal. Civil Code §1788 et seq.), which applies FDCPA-equivalent conduct rules to all debt collectors in California — including original creditors. For UA Law clients in California, Rosenthal is the hook for creditor conduct that FDCPA misses. It also allows for actual damages, statutory damages up to $1,000, and attorney fees. File Rosenthal alongside FDCPA for every California case involving a third-party collector, and file it instead of FDCPA for original creditor conduct cases in California.

The three-question framework

FDCPA asks: Who is collecting? — The FDCPA is the rulebook for third-party debt collectors. It tells them what they can and cannot do when trying to collect a consumer debt. Third-party collector? FDCPA applies.

TCPA asks: How did they contact the client, and did they still have consent? ATDS or prerecorded voice after revocation? TCPA applies to everyone — including original creditors.

Rosenthal asks: Is this a California client? If yes, Rosenthal covers original creditor conduct that the federal FDCPA misses — and applies FDCPA-equivalent rules to all collectors in the state.

FDCPA — Introduction
Fair Debt Collection Practices Act
15 U.S.C. §§1692–1692p · The federal law governing how third-party debt collectors may contact, communicate with, and pursue consumers. UA Law's FDCPA practice is built on identifying violations of this statute and filing alongside TCPA.
$1,000Statutory damages per case
1 yearStatute of limitations
+feesAttorney fees if you win

Why this law exists

Congress enacted the FDCPA in 1977 after finding that abusive, deceptive, and unfair debt collection practices were widespread — causing personal bankruptcies, marital instability, job loss, and invasions of privacy. Three stated purposes:

1
Eliminate abusive collection practices
▾

Congress found that abusive practices contribute to bankruptcies, marital instability, and job loss. The statute is designed to stop these practices at scale — not just in individual cases.

2
Give consumers tools to dispute and verify debt
▾

The law creates a structured validation system — consumers have a right to demand proof of a debt's validity before a collector can continue pursuing it. This is the foundation of the debt validation program your clients are enrolled in.

3
Protect reputable collectors from unfair competition
▾

Often overlooked — the FDCPA also levels the playing field by ensuring bad-actor collectors can't undercut ethical ones by relying on harassment tactics that ethical collectors avoid.

ℹ

FDCPA is a strict liability statute. Intent is not required for most violations. The conduct itself is what matters. The bona fide error defense exists but is narrow — repeated identical "errors" are not mistakes, they are policy.

§1692a — Definitions
Who Is Covered
The FDCPA only applies to "debt collectors" pursuing "debts" as specifically defined. Getting this right at intake is step one — if FDCPA doesn't apply, pivot to TCPA and Rosenthal analysis immediately.

Who qualifies as a "debt collector"

✓
Third-party collection agencies
▾

Any person or entity whose principal business is collecting debts owed to another, or who regularly collects debts owed to another. Classic collection agencies like Encore Capital, Midland Funding, and similar firms are the core FDCPA targets.

✓
Debt buyers — courts treat them as debt collectors
▾

Once a debt buyer purchases an account, they become the creditor — but courts have consistently held that debt buyers still qualify as "debt collectors" under the FDCPA because the debt was in default when purchased. Portfolio Recovery, LVNV Funding, and similar entities are fully covered.

✓
Collection law firms
▾

Attorneys and law firms whose principal business is debt collection. The FDCPA expressly covers them. This is why collection law firm letters must include the mini-Miranda disclosure ("this is an attempt to collect a debt").

?
Original creditor using another name — possible FDCPA
▾

If an original creditor uses a subsidiary name or trade name that implies a third party is collecting, courts may apply the FDCPA. Watch for creditors operating collection departments under a different brand. This is a common attempted workaround.

!

When in doubt, document the name on the letter or caller ID. If it doesn't match the original creditor's name on the account, research the corporate relationship — it may support FDCPA coverage.

Who is excluded from FDCPA

✗
Original creditors collecting their own debt under their own name
▾

Chase calling about a Chase account. This is the core FDCPA limitation. However, TCPA still applies if they're using an autodialer or prerecorded voice. And Rosenthal applies for California clients — covering original creditor conduct that federal FDCPA misses.

✗
Creditors collecting before default
▾

Auto finance companies and mortgage servicers collecting payments on active, non-defaulted accounts are excluded. The FDCPA is triggered after default — not during normal payment servicing.

§1692b–c
Communication Rules
The FDCPA strictly governs when, where, and how a collector may contact a consumer. Many of the most provable violations are communication violations — and they overlap with TCPA claims.

Time and place restrictions — §1692c

✗
No calls before 8:00 AM or after 9:00 PM — consumer's local time
▾

Collectors must account for time zones. An early-morning or late-evening call is a per se violation. Have clients log all call times with dates — this is one of the easiest violations to prove.

✗
No contact at the workplace if employer prohibits it
▾

If the collector knows or has reason to know that the employer prohibits such contact, it must stop. The client telling the collector "don't call me at work" is sufficient notice — no formal employer policy required.

✗
No direct contact once consumer is represented by an attorney — §1692c(a)(2)
▾

The moment a client enrolls and we notify the collector of legal representation, all direct consumer contact must stop. The collector must communicate exclusively with the attorney. Any direct call to the client after that date is a violation.

§1692c(a)(2): A debt collector may not communicate with a consumer "if the debt collector knows the consumer is represented by an attorney with respect to such debt and has knowledge of, or can readily ascertain, such attorney's name and address."
⚠

UA Law action item: Send attorney representation notice on Day 1. Date-stamp delivery. Any calls to the client after that date are clean FDCPA violations — and if the calls are autodialed, TCPA violations too.

✗
Cease communication obligation after written request
▾

A written cease-and-desist stops all collection communications. The only permitted contacts after that: (1) confirming that collection is ceasing, or (2) notifying of specific intended legal action.

!

Written vs. oral — FDCPA vs. TCPA difference: Under FDCPA, the cease-communication request must be in writing to trigger §1692c. Under TCPA, an oral "stop calling me" on a recorded call is sufficient to revoke consent. File both claims and let each statute's standard apply independently.

Contacting third parties — §1692b

  • May contact third parties only to locate the consumer — address, home phone, place of employment
  • Must not reveal the existence of the debt to any third party
  • Cannot contact the same third party more than once unless that person requests it
  • Cannot identify themselves as a debt collector unless specifically asked
§1692d, §1692e, §1692f
Prohibited Practices
Three separate sections — harassment/abuse (§d), false representations (§e), and unfair practices (§f). Each is an independent cause of action. Stack them where the facts support it.

§1692d — Harassment and abuse

✗
Repeated or continuous calls to annoy — §1692d(5)
▾

Calling repeatedly or continuously with intent to annoy, abuse, or harass is expressly prohibited. Regulation F (CFPB, 2021) created a rebuttable presumption: more than 7 calls within 7 days, or calling within 7 days of a conversation, is presumptively a violation. Have clients log every call with date and time.

✗
Threats of violence or harm
▾

Any threat of violence or harm to the consumer, their reputation, or their property. This extends to implied threats. Clients should document exact language used.

✗
Obscene or profane language
▾

Use of profane or obscene language in any communication. Clients should record calls where legally permitted, or document exact words immediately after the call.

§1692e — False or misleading representations

Sixteen specific examples are listed; courts treat the list as non-exhaustive. The "least sophisticated consumer" standard applies — would the least savvy consumer be misled?

✗
Misrepresenting the amount or character of the debt — §1692e(2)
▾

Claiming a debt is larger than it actually is, including unauthorized fees or interest. Also applies to misrepresenting a time-barred debt as legally enforceable. Pull the original account statements — if the collector's amount doesn't match, that's a §1692e(2) violation and likely a §1692f(1) unfair practices violation simultaneously.

✗
Falsely implying they are an attorney — §1692e(3)
▾

Representing or implying a communication is from an attorney when it is not. Law firm letterhead used by non-attorney collectors is the classic form of this violation.

✗
Threatening legal action not intended — §1692e(5)
▾

Threatening to sue, garnish wages, or seize property when the collector has no legal basis or actual intent. One of the most common violations. Document the exact threat language.

✗
Threat of arrest or criminal prosecution — §1692e(4)
▾

Consumer debts are civil matters. No criminal prosecution is possible for nonpayment. Any suggestion of arrest is per se false — document it immediately.

✗
Failure to identify as a debt collector — §1692e(11)
▾

The mini-Miranda: every initial written and oral communication must disclose that the caller is a debt collector attempting to collect a debt, and that information obtained will be used for that purpose. Absence is a per se violation.

§1692f — Unfair practices

✗
Collecting unauthorized amounts — §1692f(1)
▾

Collecting any amount — interest, fees, charges, expenses — not expressly authorized by the original agreement or permitted by law. Compare the collector's demand to the original account agreement.

✗
Filing suit in an improper venue — §1692i
▾

A collector may only sue in the judicial district where the consumer resides or where the contract was signed. Suing in a distant or inconvenient court is an independent FDCPA violation.

§1692g — Full Statutory Analysis
Validation of Debts
The cornerstone of the debt validation program. §1692g creates a mandatory disclosure and dispute framework. Failure to comply is a per se FDCPA violation. Know this section cold.
★

Why this matters to us: The §1692g notice is the statutory foundation for the debt validation letters your clients send. It also creates independent violations when collectors fail to send the notice, ignore a written dispute, or continue collecting during the dispute window.

The 30-day validation timeline

D0
Day 0 — First contact
The collector makes initial communication with the consumer. The 5-day clock starts. If the required disclosures are included in this first contact, the separate written notice is not required.
D5
Within 5 days — Validation notice required
Unless the required information was in the first communication, the collector must send a written notice with all five required disclosures. Missing any single element is a per se violation — no additional harm required.
D30
Within 30 days — Consumer's dispute window
The consumer has 30 days from receipt of the notice to send a written dispute. Collection may continue during this window — but cannot "overshadow or be inconsistent with" the consumer's dispute rights. Demand letters with short deadlines during this period are a common overshadowing violation.
→
After written dispute — Collection suspended
All collection activity must stop until the collector obtains and mails verification of the debt. Any call or demand letter after a timely written dispute and before verification is mailed is a clear violation.

§1692g — Full statutory text

§1692g(a)Notice of debt — required contents

Within five days after the initial communication with a consumer in connection with the collection of any debt, a debt collector shall send the consumer a written notice containing:

  1. The amount of the debt
  2. The name of the creditor to whom the debt is owed
  3. A statement that unless the consumer disputes within thirty days, the debt will be assumed to be valid
  4. A statement that if the consumer notifies the debt collector in writing within thirty days that the debt is disputed, the collector will obtain verification and mail it to the consumer
  5. A statement that upon written request within thirty days, the collector will provide the name and address of the original creditor if different from the current creditor
⚠

Missing any single element is a per se §1692g violation. Courts have also found violations for notices that technically include the information but present it in a confusing or "overshadowed" way.

§1692g(b)Disputed debts — cease collection

If the consumer notifies the collector in writing within the thirty-day period that the debt is disputed, the collector shall:

  • Cease collection of the debt or any disputed portion
  • Obtain verification of the debt or a copy of a judgment
  • Mail the verification to the consumer before resuming collection
§1692g(b) — Overshadowing clause (2006 amendment)
"Any collection activities and communication during the 30-day period may not overshadow or be inconsistent with the disclosure of the consumer's right to dispute the debt or request the name and address of the original creditor."
§1692g(c)Silence is not an admission of liability

The failure of a consumer to dispute the validity of a debt may not be construed as an admission of liability. Clients who enroll after the 30-day window has already passed have not legally admitted the debt is valid. The dispute right and courtroom defenses are separate.

§1692g(d)Legal pleadings are not "initial communications"

A formal pleading in a civil action does not trigger the 5-day validation clock. Added in 2006 to address attorney-collectors who file lawsuits as their first contact.

What a consumer can recover — §1692k(a)

$
Actual damages — no ceiling
▾

Includes emotional distress, lost wages, out-of-pocket costs, and any other actual harm. Strong documentation of the client's stress, missed work, and life disruption strengthens actual damage claims significantly.

$
Statutory damages — up to $1,000 per lawsuit
▾

No actual harm required. A technical violation alone entitles the consumer to up to $1,000. Courts have discretion based on frequency, severity, and intentionality.

!

Key limitation: The $1,000 is per lawsuit, not per violation. Multiple FDCPA violations in the same case still yield one $1,000 statutory cap. This is why TCPA is filed alongside FDCPA — TCPA stacks per call and has no case cap.

$
Attorney fees and costs — mandatory if plaintiff wins
▾

The FDCPA mandates an award of reasonable attorney fees and costs to a successful plaintiff. This fee-shifting is what makes FDCPA cases economically viable for plaintiff attorneys even on small-dollar claims.

The bona fide error defense — §1692k(c)

15 U.S.C. §1692k(c)
A debt collector may not be held liable if it shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid any such error.
⚠

Counter-strategy: Establish a pattern. If the same collector has repeated the same conduct across multiple accounts, the bona fide error defense collapses — repeated identical "errors" are policy, not mistakes.

State-Level Overlays
Mirroring States
Federal FDCPA is the floor. Mirroring-state laws are the state-level overlays. In some states, that overlay copies or imports FDCPA rules and applies them more broadly — sometimes against original creditors the federal statute cannot touch. For UA Law, this is where the FDCPA playbook gets bigger than federal FDCPA alone.
The clean explanation
Mirroring states are where state law gives consumers FDCPA-like protections — even when the federal FDCPA may not apply.
These laws often mirror the FDCPA's rules against harassment, deception, unfair practices, improper contact, and validation issues — but some states go further by applying those rules to original creditors, not just third-party debt collectors. Mirroring-state laws may let us pursue original creditors, debt buyers, collection agencies, law firms, servicers, and vendors. You need to verify each state separately before filing.
⚖
The violation unit question — reframe the intake

Do not ask only "Is this FDCPA?" Ask: "Is this federal FDCPA, state mirror law, TCPA, or all of the above?" A client call from an original creditor may be weak under federal FDCPA but strong under a state mirror law. The state of client residence at the time of the conduct is the controlling factor — not where the creditor is headquartered.

The FCRA governs who can access consumer credit reports, what information can be reported, and what happens when that information is wrong. Unlike the FDCPA, violations can come from three different actors — and the statute, the duties, and the damages path depend on which actor failed.

Why FCRA matters for debt relief clients
The debt they enrolled is almost certainly being reported. How it's being reported — and whether that reporting is accurate — is a separate set of claims.
Creditors and collectors who report inaccurate information to the credit bureaus — or who fail to investigate and correct it after a dispute — are furnisher violations under §1681s-2(b). These create a private right of action independent of FDCPA claims. Every debt relief client is a potential FCRA client. The enrollment conversation is the natural screening moment.

The three-actor framework

Every FCRA claim sits at the intersection of three parties. Understanding who failed — and at which step — determines which section of the statute applies and what the damages path looks like.

The two subsections — and why only one matters for private suits

§1681s-2(a) — Duty to Report Accurately
  • Prohibits furnishing info known to be inaccurate
  • Prohibits continuing to report info after being told it is inaccurate
  • Requires correction after furnisher learns of an error
  • No private right of action — enforced only by FTC/CFPB and state AGs
§1681s-2(b) — Post-Dispute Investigation Duties ★
  • Triggered when CRA notifies furnisher of a consumer dispute
  • Must conduct reasonable investigation of the disputed info
  • Must review all relevant info provided by the CRA
  • Must report results back to the CRA
  • Private right of action exists — this is the lawsuit hook
!

Direct disputes to furnishers (Regulation V): Under 16 C.F.R. Part 660 (Reg V), furnishers must also investigate disputes sent directly to them by the consumer — not just disputes routed through a CRA. However, the direct-dispute path to a private lawsuit is less established in most circuits. The CRA-mediated dispute path (§1681i → §1681s-2(b)) remains the cleaner litigation trigger.

What a "reasonable investigation" requires

Courts evaluate whether a furnisher's investigation was objectively reasonable given the nature of the dispute. A rubber-stamp verification is not a reasonable investigation. Here is what courts look for:

✓
Reviewed all information the CRA forwarded with the dispute
▾

The CRA sends an Automated Consumer Dispute Verification (ACDV) form containing the consumer's basis for the dispute. The furnisher must actually review the content — not just check internal records and confirm the balance matches.

Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson — a furnisher who merely checked its own records without considering the consumer's dispute basis did not conduct a reasonable investigation.
✓
Investigated the specific inaccuracy alleged — not just any inaccuracy
▾

If the consumer says the balance is wrong, the furnisher must investigate the balance — not just verify that the account exists. Scope matters. A "verified" response that doesn't address the actual dispute is not reasonable.

✓
Modified, deleted, or permanently blocked inaccurate information
▾

If the investigation finds the information is inaccurate or incomplete, the furnisher must update or delete it and report the result to the CRA. Continuing to report verified-as-inaccurate information after the investigation is a standalone violation.

✗
Re-insertion of deleted information — requires certification
▾

If a CRA previously deleted information after a dispute, a furnisher can only re-insert it if it certifies the information is complete and accurate. Re-insertion without notice to the consumer is a violation. Unauthorized re-insertion can support a willful violation claim.

⚠

Re-aging (reporting an old debt with a new "date of first delinquency") is a separate violation — it restarts the 7-year reporting clock and materially misrepresents the account status. It is common in debt-buyer portfolios.

15 U.S.C. §1681s-2(b)(1) — Furnisher Duties After Notification
After receiving notice of a dispute from a consumer reporting agency, a furnisher shall: (A) conduct an investigation with respect to the disputed information; (B) review all relevant information provided by the consumer reporting agency; (C) report the results of the investigation to the consumer reporting agency; and (D) if the investigation finds that the information is incomplete or inaccurate, report those results to all other consumer reporting agencies.

The three major CRAs — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — operate under a separate but parallel set of duties. CRA claims are typically alongside furnisher claims, not instead of them. A consumer dispute activates duties on both sides simultaneously.

Maximum possible accuracy — §1681e(b)

15 U.S.C. §1681e(b)
Whenever a consumer reporting agency prepares a consumer report it shall follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates.

This is a standalone CRA obligation independent of the dispute process. If a CRA reports information it knew — or should have known — to be inaccurate, §1681e(b) applies even without a prior dispute. Common examples:

  • Reporting a debt that belongs to someone with a similar name (mixed-file error)
  • Reporting an account discharged in bankruptcy as still open or delinquent
  • Reporting an account beyond its 7-year reporting window
  • Reporting an identity theft account after receiving a fraud alert

Right to dispute — the litigation trigger

A consumer may dispute any information they believe to be inaccurate or incomplete. The dispute letter is the single most important document in an FCRA case — it triggers the CRA's §1681i duties and the furnisher's §1681s-2(b) duties simultaneously.

Dispute letter essentials — what must be included
Identity: full name, address, SSN, date of birth — and a copy of a government ID. Identify each disputed item specifically — creditor name, account number, what is wrong, and why. State the relief sought: correction, deletion, or suppression. Send via certified mail, return receipt requested. Keep every copy.
!

Credit repair organization rules apply: If UA Law is assisting consumers with the dispute letter process, be aware that the Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA) may impose disclosure and contract requirements. Confirm with supervising counsel that the representation structure complies. Advice from a licensed attorney is exempt from most CROA requirements — the arrangement must be structured correctly.

Adverse action notice — §1681m

Any person who takes an adverse action against a consumer based in whole or in part on a credit report must provide a notice that includes: the name, address, and phone number of the CRA that provided the report; a statement that the CRA did not make the adverse action decision; the consumer's right to obtain a free report within 60 days; and the right to dispute the accuracy of the report.

Consumer's right to add a 100-word statement — §1681i(b)

If a reinvestigation does not resolve the dispute to the consumer's satisfaction, the consumer may file a brief statement — up to 100 words — with the CRA explaining their position on the disputed item. The CRA must include the substance of that statement in future reports and provide it to any party requesting the report. This is not a litigation substitute — it is a consumer protection right — but it creates a contemporaneous record useful in subsequent litigation.

⚠

The FCRA damages trap — most misunderstood issue in consumer protection: Statutory damages are only available for willful violations. A negligent violation entitles the consumer to actual damages and fees — but zero statutory damages. This is the opposite of what most people assume and is the central damages analysis in every FCRA case.

§1681n — Willful Noncompliance

Available for Willful Violations
  • Actual damages (or none if statutory elected)
  • Statutory damages: $100–$1,000 per violation
  • Punitive damages — amount at court's discretion
  • Attorney's fees and costs
  • Class actions: up to $1,000/person or 1% of net worth, capped at $500,000
Available for Negligent Violations Only
  • Actual damages (must prove and quantify)
  • No statutory damages
  • No punitive damages
  • Attorney's fees and costs

What makes a violation "willful"

Under Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr (2007), willfulness under the FCRA includes not just knowing violations but also reckless disregard of the statute. A furnisher or CRA that adopted a policy with an unjustifiably high risk of violating the FCRA — even if they did not know — can be found to have willfully violated it.

✗
Facts supporting willfulness — furnisher
▾
  • Investigation completed in hours or minutes — too fast to be genuine
  • Furnisher's standard policy is to simply match internal records without reviewing the consumer's dispute basis
  • Furnisher previously received identical disputes from other consumers on the same issue (pattern)
  • Re-insertion of previously deleted item without proper certification
  • Continuing to report a debt known to be disputed without notation
✗
Facts supporting willfulness — CRA
▾
  • Mixed-file error involving a known, long-standing data matching problem
  • CRA failed to forward the complete consumer dispute to the furnisher (truncated ACDV)
  • CRA has received regulatory action or prior litigation on the same type of error
  • Automated reinvestigation without any human review of complex disputes

Statute of limitations — §1681p

2 yrFrom date of discovery of violation
5 yrFrom date violation occurred (outer limit)
LongerDiscovery rule usually controls — document when client found out

The discovery rule means the clock starts when the consumer first knew — or reasonably should have known — about the violation. For credit reporting errors, courts typically look to when the consumer first obtained their credit report showing the inaccuracy. Document this date carefully at intake.

Proving actual damages — not automatic

For negligent violations and to support punitive damages in willful cases, actual damages must be proven. Common categories accepted by courts:
