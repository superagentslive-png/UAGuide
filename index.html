What to verify for every state before filing

Some states merely resemble the FDCPA. Some incorporate it directly. Some have entirely independent rules. Before opening any state-law claim:

  • Does the statute cover original creditors, or only third-party collectors?
  • What is the damages structure — per case cap, per violation stacking, treble, or punitive available?
  • Is there a pre-suit notice requirement? (Massachusetts Chapter 93A requires a 30-day demand letter — failure to send is a bar to the claim)
  • Are there additional prohibited conduct rules beyond the federal FDCPA list?
  • What is the state statute of limitations? Several states are more favorable than the federal 1-year clock.
  • Does the state have licensing requirements for collectors that could create additional violation hooks?
  • Are there state-specific call frequency limits beyond Regulation F's 7-in-7 presumption?

  • Debt is consumer (personal/family/household) — not business or commercial

Step 2 — §1692g validation notice violations

  • Did client receive a written validation notice within 5 days of first contact?
  • If yes — did the notice contain all 5 required elements?
  • Was the dispute right "overshadowed" by urgent short-deadline payment demands?
  • Has client sent a written dispute? If yes, did collection stop immediately?
  • Did the collector resume collection before mailing verification?

Step 3 — Communication violations §1692c

  • Send attorney representation notice Day 1 — date-stamp delivery. All direct consumer contact after that is a violation.
  • Any calls before 8:00 AM or after 9:00 PM in the client's time zone?
  • Any calls to the workplace after client requested otherwise?
  • Any contact with third parties that revealed the existence of the debt?

Step 4 — TCPA analysis

  • Signs of autodialer: dead air before agent speaks, identical robotic opener, rapid-fire call cadence?
  • Any prerecorded or AI-voice messages or voicemails?
  • Any automated texts (short code, instant response, identical message strings)?
  • Did client ever verbally say "stop calling"? On a recorded line? Document date.
  • Has attorney representation notice been sent? (Strongest consent revocation — count every call after that date)
  • Count total calls after first revocation — each post-revocation autodialed call = $500–$1,500
  • If debt was sold/assigned: treat consent chain as a discovery issue — do not assume consent is broken, but document who obtained it and whether revocation occurred
  • Is the caller a vendor or call center acting on behalf of the creditor? Vicarious liability may apply to the creditor.

Step 5 — Harassment and false statements §1692d/e

  • More than 7 calls in any 7-day period? (Regulation F presumption of harassment)
  • Any calls within 7 days of an actual conversation? (Second Regulation F presumption)
  • Any threats of arrest, criminal prosecution, or wage garnishment before a judgment?
  • Any claim that the caller is an attorney when they are not?
  • Any fees or interest added beyond what the original agreement permits?
  • Any failure to identify as a debt collector in initial communication?

Step 6 — Documentation to collect

  • Full call log from client's phone carrier (incoming calls with exact dates and times)
  • All written letters, emails, or notices received from the collector
  • All voicemails saved — do not delete, note whether prerecorded or live
  • Screenshots of any texts including short-code messages
  • Copy of original credit agreement if available
  • Account statements showing balance vs. what the collector is demanding
  • Date of attorney representation notice and confirmed delivery method

Fair Credit Reporting Act
The FCRA governs who can access consumer credit reports, what information can be reported, and what happens when that information is wrong. Unlike the FDCPA, violations can come from three different actors — and the statute, the duties, and the damages path depend on which actor failed.

Why FCRA matters for debt relief clients
The debt they enrolled is almost certainly being reported. How it's being reported — and whether that reporting is accurate — is a separate set of claims.
Creditors and collectors who report inaccurate information to the credit bureaus — or who fail to investigate and correct it after a dispute — are furnisher violations under §1681s-2(b). These create a private right of action independent of FDCPA claims. Every debt relief client is a potential FCRA client. The enrollment conversation is the natural screening moment.

The three-actor framework

Every FCRA claim sits at the intersection of three parties. Understanding who failed — and at which step — determines which section of the statute applies and what the damages path looks like.

Creditors, collectors, and debt buyers who report account data to the bureaus. If they report inaccurate info or fail to investigate a dispute — that's a §1681s-2(b) violation. Click to see furnisher rules →

The §1681s-2(b) hook: There is no private right of action under §1681s-2(a) — the general duty to report accurately. Without a dispute, there is no §1681s-2(b) claim.

The two subsections — and why only one matters for private suits

§1681s-2(a) — Duty to Report Accurately
  • Prohibits furnishing info known to be inaccurate
  • Prohibits continuing to report info after being told it is inaccurate
  • Requires correction after furnisher learns of an error
  • No private right of action — enforced only by FTC/CFPB and state AGs
§1681s-2(b) — Post-Dispute Investigation Duties ★
  • Triggered when CRA notifies furnisher of a consumer dispute
  • Must conduct reasonable investigation of the disputed info
  • Must review all relevant info provided by the CRA
  • Must report results back to the CRA
  • Private right of action exists — this is the lawsuit hook
!

Direct disputes to furnishers (Regulation V): Under 16 C.F.R. Part 660 (Reg V), furnishers must also investigate disputes sent directly to them by the consumer — not just disputes routed through a CRA. However, the direct-dispute path to a private lawsuit is less established in most circuits. The CRA-mediated dispute path (§1681i → §1681s-2(b)) remains the cleaner litigation trigger.

What a "reasonable investigation" requires

Courts evaluate whether a furnisher's investigation was objectively reasonable given the nature of the dispute. A rubber-stamp verification is not a reasonable investigation. Here is what courts look for:

✓
Reviewed all information the CRA forwarded with the dispute
▾

The CRA sends an Automated Consumer Dispute Verification (ACDV) form containing the consumer's basis for the dispute. The furnisher must actually review the content — not just check internal records and confirm the balance matches.

Gorman v. Wolpoff & Abramson — a furnisher who merely checked its own records without considering the consumer's dispute basis did not conduct a reasonable investigation.
✓
Investigated the specific inaccuracy alleged — not just any inaccuracy
▾

If the consumer says the balance is wrong, the furnisher must investigate the balance — not just verify that the account exists. Scope matters. A "verified" response that doesn't address the actual dispute is not reasonable.

✓
Modified, deleted, or permanently blocked inaccurate information
▾

If the investigation finds the information is inaccurate or incomplete, the furnisher must update or delete it and report the result to the CRA. Continuing to report verified-as-inaccurate information after the investigation is a standalone violation.

✗
Re-insertion of deleted information — requires certification
▾

If a CRA previously deleted information after a dispute, a furnisher can only re-insert it if it certifies the information is complete and accurate. Re-insertion without notice to the consumer is a violation. Unauthorized re-insertion can support a willful violation claim.

⚠

Re-aging (reporting an old debt with a new "date of first delinquency") is a separate violation — it restarts the 7-year reporting clock and materially misrepresents the account status. It is common in debt-buyer portfolios.

15 U.S.C. §1681s-2(b)(1) — Furnisher Duties After Notification
After receiving notice of a dispute from a consumer reporting agency, a furnisher shall: (A) conduct an investigation with respect to the disputed information; (B) review all relevant information provided by the consumer reporting agency; (C) report the results of the investigation to the consumer reporting agency; and (D) if the investigation finds that the information is incomplete or inaccurate, report those results to all other consumer reporting agencies.

The three major CRAs — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — operate under a separate but parallel set of duties. CRA claims are typically alongside furnisher claims, not instead of them. A consumer dispute activates duties on both sides simultaneously.

Maximum possible accuracy — §1681e(b)

15 U.S.C. §1681e(b)
Whenever a consumer reporting agency prepares a consumer report it shall follow reasonable procedures to assure maximum possible accuracy of the information concerning the individual about whom the report relates.

This is a standalone CRA obligation independent of the dispute process. If a CRA reports information it knew — or should have known — to be inaccurate, §1681e(b) applies even without a prior dispute. Common examples:

  • Reporting a debt that belongs to someone with a similar name (mixed-file error)
  • Reporting an account discharged in bankruptcy as still open or delinquent
  • Reporting an account beyond its 7-year reporting window
  • Reporting an identity theft account after receiving a fraud alert

Mixed-file errors — highest-severity CRA violations

⚠

Mixed files occur when a CRA merges credit data from two different consumers — typically people with similar names, SSNs, or addresses — onto a single report. These are among the most damaging FCRA violations because the consumer's file contains someone else's negative history and the harm is immediate. Mixed-file cases often support punitive damages claims because CRAs are well aware of the problem and have been put on notice.

During COVID, weekly free reports became available; that policy has been extended periodically. Free reports are also available after an adverse action, during active fraud alert, if unemployed and seeking employment, or if receiving public assistance.

Right to dispute — the litigation trigger

A consumer may dispute any information they believe to be inaccurate or incomplete. The dispute letter is the single most important document in an FCRA case — it triggers the CRA's §1681i duties and the furnisher's §1681s-2(b) duties simultaneously.

Dispute letter essentials — what must be included
Identity: full name, address, SSN, date of birth — and a copy of a government ID. Identify each disputed item specifically — creditor name, account number, what is wrong, and why. State the relief sought: correction, deletion, or suppression. Send via certified mail, return receipt requested. Keep every copy.
!

Credit repair organization rules apply: If UA Law is assisting consumers with the dispute letter process, be aware that the Credit Repair Organizations Act (CROA) may impose disclosure and contract requirements. Confirm with supervising counsel that the representation structure complies. Advice from a licensed attorney is exempt from most CROA requirements — the arrangement must be structured correctly.

Adverse action notice — §1681m

Any person who takes an adverse action against a consumer based in whole or in part on a credit report must provide a notice that includes: the name, address, and phone number of the CRA that provided the report; a statement that the CRA did not make the adverse action decision; the consumer's right to obtain a free report within 60 days; and the right to dispute the accuracy of the report.

!

No private right of action under §1681m: A missing or defective adverse-action notice is a regulatory/compliance violation — but §1681m generally does not create a private damages claim. Document it as part of the factual story and to support actual damages, but do not represent it to clients as a standalone lawsuit. Confirm with supervising counsel before asserting any §1681m-based private claim.

Security freeze and fraud alert rights — §1681c-1/c-2

  • Consumer may place a free security freeze at all three major CRAs — blocks most new credit inquiries
  • Initial fraud alert: 1-year duration; requires CRAs to notify all other CRAs within 2 days
  • Extended fraud alert: 7 years — requires documentation of identity theft
  • Active duty alert: 1-year — available to military members on active duty
  • CRAs must provide a free credit report when a fraud alert is placed

Consumer's right to add a 100-word statement — §1681i(b)

If a reinvestigation does not resolve the dispute to the consumer's satisfaction, the consumer may file a brief statement — up to 100 words — with the CRA explaining their position on the disputed item. The CRA must include the substance of that statement in future reports and provide it to any party requesting the report. This is not a litigation substitute — it is a consumer protection right — but it creates a contemporaneous record useful in subsequent litigation.

⚠

The FCRA damages trap — most misunderstood issue in consumer protection: Statutory damages are only available for willful violations. A negligent violation entitles the consumer to actual damages and fees — but zero statutory damages. This is the opposite of what most people assume and is the central damages analysis in every FCRA case.

§1681n — Willful Noncompliance

Available for Willful Violations
  • Actual damages (or none if statutory elected)
  • Statutory damages: $100–$1,000 per violation
  • Punitive damages — amount at court's discretion
  • Attorney's fees and costs
  • Class actions: up to $1,000/person or 1% of net worth, capped at $500,000
Available for Negligent Violations Only
  • Actual damages (must prove and quantify)
  • No statutory damages
  • No punitive damages
  • Attorney's fees and costs

What makes a violation "willful"

Under Safeco Insurance Co. of America v. Burr (2007), willfulness under the FCRA includes not just knowing violations but also reckless disregard of the statute. A furnisher or CRA that adopted a policy with an unjustifiably high risk of violating the FCRA — even if they did not know — can be found to have willfully violated it.

✗
Facts supporting willfulness — furnisher
▾
  • Investigation completed in hours or minutes — too fast to be genuine
  • Furnisher's standard policy is to simply match internal records without reviewing the consumer's dispute basis
  • Furnisher previously received identical disputes from other consumers on the same issue (pattern)
  • Re-insertion of previously deleted item without proper certification
  • Continuing to report a debt known to be disputed without notation
✗
Facts supporting willfulness — CRA
▾
  • Mixed-file error involving a known, long-standing data matching problem
  • CRA failed to forward the complete consumer dispute to the furnisher (truncated ACDV)
  • CRA has received regulatory action or prior litigation on the same type of error
  • Automated reinvestigation without any human review of complex disputes

Statute of limitations — §1681p

2 yrFrom date of discovery of violation
5 yrFrom date violation occurred (outer limit)
LongerDiscovery rule usually controls — document when client found out

The discovery rule means the clock starts when the consumer first knew — or reasonably should have known — about the violation. For credit reporting errors, courts typically look to when the consumer first obtained their credit report showing the inaccuracy. Document this date carefully at intake.
